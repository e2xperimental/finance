The key idea is that market-cap weighting lets you own the market without having to predict which companies will be the winners. But it doesn't guarantee that the market will always go up, and it isn't the only way to construct an index.

Market capitalization is the total market value of a company's outstanding shares.

For example:

- Apple has a large market value because investors collectively value its shares highly.
- A much smaller company has a lower market value.
- A market-cap-weighted index allocates more of its money to Apple and less to the smaller company.

The S&P 500 and VOO use float-adjusted market-cap weighting, while VTI tracks the broad U.S. stock market using a similar market-cap-weighted approach.

Here's a simplified example of a $10,000 portfolio.

## Hypothetical market-cap-weighted portfolio

Three companies with different market values

Company A

Market cap: $1 trillion

### 80%

$8,000

Company B

Market cap: $200 billion

### 16%

$1,600

Company C

Market cap: $50 billion

### 4%

$400

Why do this instead of investing equal amounts in all three companies?

There are four main reasons. It:

1. Reflects the market's collective valuation. You aren't betting that a small company will outperform a large one.
2. Scales naturally. As a company's market value grows relative to others, its weight increases automatically.
3. Generally requires less trading. Price movements adjust the weights without requiring you to sell winners and buy losers just to restore fixed percentages.
4. Reduces the need for subjective decisions. You don't have to decide which companies deserve a bigger allocation.

There is a subtle point here: market-cap weighting doesn't mean the biggest companies are necessarily the best investments. It means their market values determine their representation in the index.

The relationship between market-cap weighting and Collins' philosophy is important.

### Why market-cap weighting can benefit from long-term economic growth

Imagine three companies:

- Company A becomes increasingly successful and its market value rises.
- Company B grows moderately.
- Company C loses ground.

In a market-cap-weighted index, Company A naturally becomes a larger part of your portfolio. Company C becomes a smaller part.

You don't need to identify Company A in advance. If it grows enough to become a major company, you automatically own more of it relative to the rest of the index.

There is another benefit: when companies become less successful, their weights shrink, and companies that grow into major businesses can eventually join the index.

This helps explain why broad-market investing can be effective over long periods.

However, there are two important qualifications:

- The index does not sell a declining stock just because its price falls. It usually keeps holding it until it no longer qualifies or another index rule triggers a change.
- Long-term growth is not guaranteed. Stocks have experienced prolonged periods of disappointing returns, and even broad U.S. indexes can suffer substantial declines.

Market-cap weighting is a way to participate in market growth. It isn't a mechanism that guarantees recovery by a particular date.

### The historical lesson: the market can recover, but the path can be rough

Collins' approach depends on a combination of economic growth, business earnings, reinvested dividends, and patience. It does not depend on every stock going up.

A market-cap-weighted index benefits when successful businesses grow, even if other businesses stagnate or fail. However, a broad index can still be heavily concentrated in a few very large companies.

For example, S&P Dow Jones Indices reported that its top 10 companies accounted for 37.8% of the S&P 500's weight as of August 31, 2026.&#x20;

## 3. Are there index funds that aren't market-cap weighted?

Absolutely. There are several alternatives, and each makes a different trade-off.

[(RSP) Invesco S&P 500 Equal Weight ETF ETF performance metrics – Composer](https://images.openai.com/static-rsc-4/F0gFsuhGBiacU3F6czxE1OUDSoAJZp4sLyB0uXAaHh_QbOpBXZ0GLR-D3A9fPETNYvKZvsbr_BxsAiO78waeBn3cDIUmKiAJLd6-n_Z-tVtNnQxWUva_HnDD67083ZqPBCETmzdh6xrM58hJgShK9hWsFYo3qeZMiC6wSLrgFKE?purpose=inline)

A. Equal-weighted indexes

Example: RSP.

Every S&P 500 company gets approximately 0.20% at each rebalance, rather than the largest companies dominating the portfolio.

- Advantage: Less concentration in the mega-cap stocks.
- Trade-off: Requires more rebalancing, typically has higher turnover, and gives smaller S&P 500 companies much more influence.

B. Fundamental-weighted indexes

Example: FNDX.

Weight companies using business measures such as sales, cash flow, dividends, and book value, rather than relying primarily on stock-market value.

- Advantage: Can reduce reliance on companies whose stock prices have risen far ahead of their business fundamentals.
- Trade-off: Can underperform for long periods if expensive growth stocks keep leading.

C. Factor and value indexes

Example: VTV.

These select or weight companies according to characteristics such as valuation, profitability, momentum, or volatility. Some use market-cap weighting within the selected group; others use alternative weighting rules.

- Advantage: Can systematically emphasize particular investment characteristics.
- Trade-off: You are making a deliberate bet that those characteristics will be rewarded.

[DJIA - Dow Jones Industrial Average acronym](https://images.openai.com/static-rsc-4/hKZKRQO3mhx8dZoeiGnrzznDZF3GGt8vENxiNCzBLhEuZlluRsiI-pmlmOZl6dDMAqh7a9Fj-UprkvzPGWJcZ66Vppn1XPnJRbiGnANRV162BDnUU8nYpFHqOYdKrkT1CYLKeT7GCY4O57SNd-B6VUBa-UdJ1iXS7SQdV0N3267EbI2JxeR57fuj2ZpMll0y?purpose=inline)

D. Price-weighted indexes

Example: DIA.

The Dow Jones Industrial Average gives more weight to stocks with higher share prices, not companies with higher total market values.

- Advantage: An established alternative index construction method.
- Trade-off: A company's share price alone is not a particularly meaningful measure of its economic importance.

These are genuine alternatives, not just theoretical ideas. S&P Dow Jones Indices documents equal weighting, factor weighting, strategic tilts, and other non-market-cap approaches in its index mathematics methodology.&#x20;

## 4. Which approach is most likely to go up over the long term?

Here's the distinction I would keep in mind:

| Strategy                   | What you're betting on                                         | Main risk                                                |
| -------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------- |
| VTI — total U.S. market    | U.S. businesses collectively grow                              | Broad U.S. market decline                                |
| VOO — S&P 500              | Large U.S. companies grow                                      | Large-company concentration                              |
| RSP — equal-weight S&P 500 | Smaller S&P 500 companies do well relative to the biggest ones | Smaller companies lag, or rebalancing costs hurt returns |
| Fundamental weighting      | Business fundamentals matter more than market prices           | Growth stocks outperform fundamentals-based strategies   |
| Value indexes              | Relatively inexpensive companies outperform                    | Value stocks underperform for years                      |

No weighting method can guarantee that stocks will rise indefinitely. Equal weighting, for example, may outperform when smaller companies lead, but it can lag significantly when the largest companies dominate the market.

For comparison, the SEC's Investor.gov explains that factor-based and other nontraditional index funds can target characteristics such as value, financial strength, or other investment criteria, but they introduce different risks from traditional broad-market indexes.&#x20;

## 5. My recommendation for your DIY index experiment

Given your interest in building your own stock portfolio and minimizing expenses, I would compare three approaches before spending time maintaining hundreds of individual stocks.

Portfolio A: Market-cap weighted

Use VTI or a DIY portfolio modeled on its holdings. This is the closest match to the broad-market approach Collins advocates.

Portfolio B: Equal-weighted

Use RSP as a benchmark, or construct a portfolio with equal weights and periodic rebalancing. This tests whether reducing mega-cap concentration helps.

Portfolio C: Your custom index

Choose a rules-based combination of market capitalization, value, or profitability. Compare its returns against VTI and VOO, after estimated trading costs and taxes.

I would evaluate each portfolio over multiple periods, including years when growth stocks underperformed, and measure total returns with dividends reinvested, maximum drawdown, turnover, and tax consequences.

One final consideration: VOO's expense ratio is only 0.03% per year. That's $30 annually on $100,000 invested.&#x20;
